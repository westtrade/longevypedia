## Definition

Biological aging is the progressive loss of function that accumulates with age
and increases vulnerability to disease and death. It is distinct from
chronological age: two people of the same age can differ substantially in
physiological state, and that difference predicts mortality better than the
calendar does.

The distinction is not merely semantic. If aging is a set of processes, those
processes are candidates for measurement and, in principle, for intervention.
If it is only the passage of time, nothing can be done.

## The hallmarks framework

The most widely used map of the field groups aging processes into a set of
interconnected "hallmarks", revised and expanded in 2023. They include genomic
instability, telomere attrition, epigenetic alterations, loss of proteostasis,
disabled macroautophagy, mitochondrial dysfunction,
[[mechanisms/cellular-senescence|cellular senescence]], stem-cell exhaustion,
altered intercellular communication, chronic inflammation and dysbiosis.

The framework is a taxonomy, not a mechanism. It says where to look; it does not
say which hallmark drives the others, and different tissues give different
answers. Treat the list as a shared vocabulary rather than as an established
causal hierarchy.

## What the evidence supports

Three claims are well supported across model organisms:

- Aging is plastic. Caloric restriction, single-gene mutations and
  pharmacological interventions extend lifespan in yeast, worms, flies and
  mice.
- The effects are conserved in part, not entirely. Pathways such as insulin/IGF-1
  and mTOR appear repeatedly, but their direction and magnitude differ by
  species and tissue.
- Healthspan and lifespan can be uncoupled. Interventions exist that extend one
  without the other, in both directions.

## What it does not yet support

There is no validated intervention that slows biological aging in healthy
humans. Candidate biomarkers of biological age — epigenetic clocks, proteomic
scores, composite clinical indices — correlate with age and predict mortality in
cohorts, but correlation with age is not the same as measuring the rate of
aging, and none has been qualified as a surrogate endpoint for a trial.
