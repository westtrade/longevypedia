## Definition

Healthspan is the period of life spent in good health. Unlike
[[concepts/longevity|lifespan]], it has no single agreed measurement, because
"good health" is not a laboratory value. Studies operationalise it in three
broad ways:

1. **Disease-free survival** — time until the first diagnosis of a defined list
   of age-related diseases.
2. **Functional measures** — gait speed, grip strength, cognitive test scores,
   and composite frailty indices.
3. **Compression of morbidity** — whether the total time spent in poor health
   before death shrinks, even if lifespan grows.

The third is the one that matters for policy and for individuals, and it is the
hardest to estimate: it requires knowing both when disability begins and when
death occurs, in the same cohort.

## Why the distinction matters

An intervention that extends life by extending the period of frailty increases
the burden on the individual and on health systems. An intervention that
extends life by delaying the onset of disease does the opposite. Both can show
up as the same improvement in a median-lifespan figure, which is why
[[concepts/biological-aging|aging biology]] increasingly treats healthspan
measures as primary outcomes rather than as secondary notes.

## Measurement in practice

In animal research, healthspan is assessed with composite batteries: rotarod and
grip strength for motor function, novel object recognition for memory, glucose
tolerance for metabolic health, and standardised frailty indices for mice. No
single test is decisive, and a treatment that improves one while worsening
another is a common result.

In humans, the equivalent data come from cohort studies and from trials that
were designed for something else. This is a structural limitation, not an
oversight: healthy people are hard to randomise to a decades-long intervention.
