## Definition

Longevity is the length of life of an organism. In human research it is usually
described with one of three measures:

- **Lifespan** — the age at death of an individual, or the distribution of ages
  at death in a population.
- **Median lifespan** — the age by which half of a cohort has died. Less
  sensitive than the maximum to a handful of exceptional individuals.
- **Maximum lifespan** — the age of the oldest individual observed. A single
  record-holder, so it is a weak statistic on its own.

A fourth measure, **life expectancy at birth**, is a population average that
depends mostly on infant and early-adult mortality. Improvements in life
expectancy over the twentieth century came largely from sanitation, vaccination
and the treatment of infectious disease, not from slowing aging itself.

## Longevity is not healthspan

Living longer and living well are different outcomes, and they can diverge. A
treatment that extends lifespan while prolonging a period of disability is a
poor result for the individual, even though it looks like success in a survival
curve. This is why aging research increasingly reports
[[concepts/healthspan|healthspan]] — the fraction of life spent free of serious
disease — alongside lifespan, and why frailty and functional measures are now
common secondary endpoints in animal studies.

## What determines longevity

Longevity is a polygenic trait with a large environmental component. In humans,
heritability of lifespan is estimated at roughly 20–30%, and the heritability of
*exceptional* longevity is higher but still incomplete. The strongest
single-gene effects found in humans are modest in size; the best-replicated
candidate is a variant in [[genes/foxo3|FOXO3]], first associated with longevity
in long-lived cohorts of Japanese ancestry.

In model organisms the picture is different: single-gene mutations can extend
lifespan several-fold in yeast, worms and flies, and substantially in mice. That
difference is a warning about extrapolation, not evidence that human aging is
unmodifiable.

## Measures and their limits

Survival curves summarise a cohort, not a mechanism. Two interventions can
produce identical median lifespan extensions through entirely different
processes — one by delaying the onset of age-related disease, another by
improving recovery from it. Distinguishing the two requires age-specific
mortality rates, cause-of-death data and physiological measures, not just a
final number.
